Epic #208 · Design brainstorm · August 2, 2026

Finalizing an election

How results enter the data, how we know they are final, and what a completed race looks like on the site — sized to a guide whose job is helping people vote, not covering the count.

What the site wants to beThe decision that sizes everything else

The site's purpose is in the name: it helps voters vote. Results are a secondary feature — but "secondary" still has three plausible sizes, and choosing one settles most of the open questions downstream.

A · Point elsewhere

Too little

The archived guide gains one line — "this election has concluded; see King County Elections for results" — and nothing else changes. Cheap, but it leaves the archive permanently saying "vote for X" about a question that has been answered, and the reader has to leave to find out how the story ended.

B · Close the record

Decided · Aug 2, 2026

Certified results are ingested once, with the same evidence-capture and provenance discipline as endorsements, and each archived race states how it completed: who advanced or won, with what share, certified when, by whom. While ballots are being counted the site links out rather than republishing numbers. The archive becomes a complete historical record; the site never becomes a results tracker.

C · Track the count

Too much

Ingest election-night numbers and the daily ballot drops, updating races as counts evolve. This competes with King County's own results site (which will always be fresher), demands deploy operations exactly when attention is scarcest, and — because late ballots shift Seattle results substantially — publishes numbers most likely to mislead precisely when traffic is highest.

Posture B also honors the epic's framing constraint: results are shown as what happened, next to what the guide recommended — never as a score. No accuracy language, no track record, no "the guide called it." The one place results genuinely serve the voting mission is continuity: a primary's outcome defines the general's ballot, and an archive that records advancement explains the next guide's starting lineup.

Already banked: election-night and certified results capture are O11 calendar milestones, deliberately separated from this design — the raw evidence will exist whenever this design lands, because those windows are unrecoverable. The calendar initially began coverage after this primary; the 2026 primary is being added retroactively, so its August 4 election-night and ~August 19 certification captures are tracked. Election night is in two days.

How finality actually arrivesWashington's count, as a state machine

Washington votes by mail with postmark-day validity, so the count is a process, not an event. The model needs four states — and only one of them publishes numbers on the site.

  • Tuesday, August 4 · election day Guide banner flips to its "past" state Status: counting. The self-retiring banner (#192) already plans for this; its stylesheet notes the past state "is where results belong later."
  • ~8:15 p.m. election night Capture №1 — election-night snapshot (evidence only) First county tabulation, hashed into the evidence store per O11. Proposed: never rendered — Seattle's late ballots routinely move races by several points, and archiving a misleading number as if it were an answer works against the site's whole posture.
  • August 5–18 · daily ballot drops Site links out; ingests nothing The counting banner names the authority and links to its live counts. No deploys, no refresh cadence, no race with the county's website.
  • ~August 19 · county certification Capture №2 — certified results (the published dataset) Status: certified. County canvass certifies roughly two weeks after a primary (three after a general). One adapter run, one review, one deploy through the normal approval gate. This is the finalization moment.
  • Rare · recount or amended canvass Status: amended — a re-run of capture №2 Mandatory recounts trigger on very close margins. Modeled as a new capture superseding the old, with the change noted in the corrections log rather than silently overwritten.

MockupsBuilt with the site's real tokens and a real race

1 · The banner carries the status

The election-day banner (#192) already exists, with its own verbiage, coloring, and self-retiring state machine: a tense-neutral server rendering, an amber escalation inside seven days, and a past-tense rewrite once the election has happened. None of that changes in this epic. The rows below reproduce the shipped states verbatim so the scope boundary is unmistakable: this design only adds what happens after election day, and only when results data exists — with no results file, the banner keeps its existing past rewrite forever.

The two new states extend the same element and the same two color surfaces — counting on the existing amber attention family, certified settling into the existing muted past state; no new hues. Their text follows four rules, because less is more: at most two lines; a line never breaks mid-sentence — each line is a complete thought; links name their destination; and exactly one date appears — the most important next one, or the last one once the election is completely over. Nothing about when counts update: that is not how this site works.

2 · A certified race card

The card keeps its identity — office, the guide's recommendation, the consensus meter — and grows a results strip below a rule. Tally bars are navy on the neutral track, deliberately outside the agree/differ tone families: results are facts, not judgments. The recommendation is not restyled by the outcome in either direction.

Between election day and certification, the same slot renders one honest line instead of numbers: a Counting — results certify Aug 19 chip in the amber family, with no tally rows. The card never shows a number the county could contradict tomorrow.

2a · A certified measure race card Ratified · Aug 8, 2026

A ballot measure reuses this exact tally-row component — no new UI mechanism, no new template block, no new CSS class. Its two choices, Yes and No, are simply its outcomes: two tally rows on the same navy/gray bar scale, sorted by share descending, exactly like a candidate race with two candidates. The winning row carries the same chip shown in "Chip vocabulary" below — Approved when Yes wins, Rejected when No wins — never red/green valence, the same fact-toned sky-on-navy as every other results chip. A rejected measure's "No" row therefore sorts above "Yes," the same share-descending rule a candidate race uses. No validation threshold is ever rendered, and none is added to the data model: the chip states a fact taken directly from the certification, the same posture advanced already carries for candidate races.

This applies identically wherever the tally-row component is consumed — the race card above, the endorsements dialog below, and the comparison column's own cell rendering — "reuse everywhere," not "race-card-only." No implementation lands in this design pass; it is tracked as a follow-up sub-issue of #208.

3 · The endorsements view

The race-detail dialog is where a reader compares candidates source by source, so results belong here too — but as a state, not an option. No toggle, no new filter control: when certified data exists for the election, the view renders it; before that, it doesn't. One state driven by the data's presence keeps the controls uncluttered and adds one rendering case to test instead of a combinatorial option.

Three touches carry it. A certified strip sits under the dialog header. Each candidate then gets a slim vote-share row between the heading and its source list — the same navy-on-track tally bar the race card uses, so votes look the same everywhere and never borrow the meter, which here already means "share of endorsing sources." The meter keeps its top-line place beside the candidate's name, with the endorsing-source count captioned beneath it. And the advancing candidates carry one small "Advances" chip immediately after the candidate's name — the same placement the race card uses, which also keeps the vote bars on one shared scale instead of shortening whichever rows happen to carry a chip. The chip is sky on navy, a lightened tint of the site's own navy, deliberately outside the agree/differ tone families. Neither good nor bad; fact.

At narrow widths nothing new is invented: the heading's two sides stack — kicker, name, and chip first; meter and source count following — and when the name and chip cannot share a line, the chip wraps beneath the name. That is the whole mobile accommodation.

The chip's label follows the race type — one component, three words. "Advances" in a primary; "Elected" once a general certifies a winner ("Won" reads like sport, and "Elected" survives contact with judges and school boards); "Approved" or "Rejected" for ballot measures, matching the language on the certification itself. Rejected stays in the same sky tint — it is still just a fact.

4 · The comparison view

The comparison page's native grammar is choosing columns, so results join /compare/ as another addable column — the one surface where showing results is a reader's choice, because column selection is that page's idiom. The state gate still governs availability: the column picker offers "Certified result" only when the results file exists. Cells speak the table's own language — choice labels on the picks line, shares and the certification status on the meta line.

One hard rule from the framing: the result column is excluded from agreement. Its cells never tint agree or differ, and it never contributes to a row's "Differs" marker. The page states what happened next to what sources said; it does not score one against the other.

5 · The corrections log

Bundled here by decision D2 because both features are the archive talking about itself after publication. Corrections are per-election: a top-line page with the same affordances and structure as any other major page, rendered — and linked in the nav — only when that election actually has corrections. Anything that crosses elections is a site matter and belongs to the changelog (O8), not to Corrections. A results amendment is the clearest case of a correction the page must carry; the editorial line is "anything that changes what a published page asserts" — routine pre-election data refreshes don't qualify; changed recommendations, retracted endorsements, and amended results do.

The data, sketchedSame discipline as endorsements

One results file per election, written by a collection adapter over the King County (and, for state races, Secretary of State) published results — evidence-captured, hashed, and validated like everything else. The election-night capture lives in the manifest but feeds no rendering.

# data/results/wa-2026-primary.yaml
election_id: wa-2026-primary
status: certified          # counting | certified | amended
certified_on: 2026-08-19
authority: King County Elections
captures:
  - kind: election_night   # evidence only, never rendered
    captured_at: 2026-08-04T20:35:00-07:00
    evidence: data/manifests/evidence/capture-kc-results-…json
  - kind: certified
    captured_at: 2026-08-19T16:05:00-07:00
    evidence: data/manifests/evidence/capture-kc-results-…json
races:
  - race_id: king-county-council-8
    ballots_counted: 61234
    outcomes:
      - choice_id: king-county-council-8--teresa-mosqueda
        votes: 33189
        share: 0.542
        advanced: true      # `elected` in a general; `approved` on measures

Validation asserts every choice_id exists in the inventory, shares sum to ~1 per race, and an amended file cites the capture it supersedes — which is what feeds the corrections log automatically.

DecisionsRatified August 2, 2026 · what little remains open

Posture B — close the record. Certified results only; the site never tracks the count.
Election-night numbers do not render. Captured as evidence, shown nowhere. Not right now; revisit only if a real need appears.
Outcomes never displace recommendations. The site is about who should win — and should have won. Reality is a secondary consideration, rendered as context beneath the recommendation, never as the headline.
Endorsements view: certified strip + per-candidate vote row. No per-source annotations. Renders as a state when certified data exists — no toggle, no new control.
Measures say Approved / Rejected. Matching the certification's own language, in the same fact-toned chip.
Corrections are per-election. A top-line page with full page affordances, existing only when that election has corrections; cross-election fixes belong to the site changelog, not Corrections. Tagline: "We get it right, eventually."
The general never consumes primary results. They are separate elections; the county's official general inventory is the sole source of truth.
Trends across elections are out of scope. Entirely. Nothing in this design anticipates them beyond the certified data existing.
Results join the comparison view as an addable column. Offered in the column picker only when certified data exists; cells use the table's own grammar; excluded from agreement — no tints and no "Differs" against reality.
Banners are terse, and pre-election states are untouched. The shipped #192 banner (tense-neutral rendering, amber escalation, past rewrite) is out of scope; the two new states activate only when results data exists and reuse its existing tones. New-state text: at most two lines; a line never breaks mid-sentence; links name their destination; exactly one date — the most important next one, or the last once the election is over. Nothing about count-update cadence.
Still open "Elected" as the general-race chip label (standing proposal, unobjected); the exact amended-result flow, decided when a recount first happens.

Ballot measuresRatified August 8, 2026 · #289

Measures reuse the candidate tally-row component, unmodified. No new UI mechanism, template block, or CSS class: a measure's Yes/No choices are just its outcomes, rendered on the same navy/gray bar scale, sorted by share descending like any race.
The winning row carries Approved / Rejected, same fact-toned chip. Approved when Yes wins, Rejected when No wins — the same sky-on-navy tone as every other results chip, never red/green valence. A rejected measure's "No" row sorts above "Yes" by the same share rule; flagged and accepted, not overlooked.
No validation thresholds are rendered, and none are added to the schema. The chip states a fact taken directly from the certification, the same posture advanced already carries for candidate races — closing this design's own "if thresholds are to be rendered" branch without a schema follow-up.
Reuse everywhere, not race-card-only. Applies identically to the race card, the endorsements dialog, and the comparison column's own cell rendering.
